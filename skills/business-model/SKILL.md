# BUSINESS MODEL SKILL

## Purpose
Design and test business models using customer value, monetization architecture, delivery economics, cash conversion and strategic fit.

## Inputs
Customer/problem; desired outcome; offer; price/packaging; CAC and unit economics when available; delivery capacity; retention; constraints; evidence quality.

## Process
1. Define customer and valuable problem.
2. Assess market quality before optimizing persuasion.
3. Map desired outcome and time-to-value.
4. Diagnose value levers: outcome, probability, delay, effort.
5. Design candidate offer and packaging.
6. Evaluate core, upsell, downsell, continuity and DIY/DWY/DFY patterns.
7. Model economics, capacity and downside.
8. Define smallest credible experiment.
9. Define primary metric, guardrails and review trigger.

## Guardrails
No deceptive scarcity or urgency; no unsupported guarantees; no assumed margin/cash-conversion targets without evidence; do not optimize revenue at the expense of customer outcome or sustainability.

## Evaluation
Pass when evidence and assumptions are separated, market/offer/economics are tested separately, and a measurable experiment is proposed.

## Value of Information (VoI) decision protocol

When several uncertainties compete for attention, do not select one automatically.
Compare the available options explicitly before recommending a priority.

For each uncertainty, record:
1. Decision impact: which concrete decision could change when this uncertainty is resolved?
2. Information value: how much could resolving it improve the decision or reduce the risk of a costly mistake?
3. Evidence cost: what time, money, effort, or access is required to obtain useful evidence?
4. Actionability: can the result change what we do next?
5. Urgency and reversibility: what is the cost of delaying the decision, and can the decision be reversed?
6. Residual uncertainty: what important unknowns would remain after the proposed test?

Compare uncertainties using the same factors and explain material trade-offs.
Do not invent numerical scores, probabilities, costs, or thresholds when evidence is unavailable.
Use qualitative comparisons when appropriate and label assumptions explicitly.

If the available evidence does not support a unique priority, state that the ranking is indeterminate.
Identify the missing information that would most efficiently distinguish the alternatives.
Recommend a small, credible evidence-gathering experiment only when its expected decision value justifies its cost.

Before finalizing, check:
- Were at least two relevant uncertainties compared when at least two are available?
- Is the selected priority supported by explicit factors rather than habit or position in a list?
- Are facts, assumptions, and unresolved unknowns distinguished?
- Is the next experiment capable of changing a concrete decision?
- What evidence would make this prioritization incorrect?
