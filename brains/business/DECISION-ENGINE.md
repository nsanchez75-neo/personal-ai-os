## Procedure

1. Define the desired business outcome.
2. Identify customer and problem.
3. Establish market/customer evidence.
4. Separate facts from assumptions.
5. Identify the critical hypothesis.
6. Classify decision B0–B3.
7. Generate alternatives, including do nothing and smaller tests.
8. Evaluate customer value, economics, capacity and downside.
9. Design the smallest credible experiment.
10. Define evidence threshold before running it.
11. Run and record the test.
12. Compare result with prediction.
13. Decide: scale, iterate, stop, defer or pivot.
14. Update the business model only when evidence justifies it.

## Hypothesis quality

A useful hypothesis states:
- what must be true;
- for whom;
- under what conditions;
- by what observable behavior;
- by when;
- what result would count as supporting/disconfirming evidence.

## Experiment quality

A test should state:
- hypothesis;
- target segment;
- intervention;
- expected behavior;
- baseline;
- sample/context;
- evidence threshold;
- cost;
- time;
- confounders;
- result;
- next decision.

## Evidence ladder

Prefer, when appropriate:
1. transaction/behavior;
2. repeated customer behavior;
3. direct experience evidence;
4. structured interview;
5. survey/stated preference;
6. anecdote/opinion.

No evidence class is universally sufficient; match evidence strength to decision stakes.

## Scaling gate

Before scaling an acquisition or offer system ask:

Customer value proven?
Retention/outcome acceptable?
Unit economics acceptable?
Delivery capacity adequate?
Operational failure modes known?
Measurement reliable?
Experiment result repeatable enough for the stakes?
Downside bounded?
Human approval satisfied?

If critical answers are unknown, scaling should be treated as a decision under uncertainty rather than an automatic next step.
